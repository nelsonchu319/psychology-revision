Key Notes

Textbook concepts from OpenStax Psychology 2e (paraphrased). Important ideas that appear in the book belong here—not in Further Depth.

1.1 What Is Psychology? OpenStax Psychology 2e §1.1, ≈p. 8

  • Psychology = scientific study of the mind and behavior.
  • Students learn critical thinking, the scientific method, and that behavior is complex (not reducible to one-liners).
  • Empiricism: knowledge about mind/behavior comes from observation and evidence, not authority alone.

中文補充：OpenStax 定義是 mind and behavior；重點在「用科學方法」而非只靠直覺。

1.2 History of Psychology §1.2, ≈pp. 8–18

  • Before Wundt/James, mind questions were largely philosophical; they helped make psychology a distinct science.
  • Wundt (Leipzig, 1879): first psychology lab; introspection to break conscious experience into parts → associated with structuralism (Titchener developed structuralism further).
  • William James / functionalism: how mental activities help an organism adapt; whole-mind function over isolated parts; used introspection plus more objective measures.
  • Freud / psychoanalytic theory: unconscious mind and early experience matter for conscious behavior (hysterias/neuroses); methods include dream analysis, slips of the tongue, free association; long clinical influence.
  • Gestalt (Wertheimer, Koffka, Köhler): experience as organized wholes; ideas still shape sensation/perception work.
  • Behaviorism: make psychology objective by studying overt behavior; Watson often called father of behaviorism; Skinner developed operant conditioning (reinforcement/punishment). Classical conditioning lineage (Pavlov) also appears in this historical arc.
  • Humanism (Maslow, Rogers): potential for good; hierarchy of needs; client-centered therapy and unconditional positive regard.
  • Cognitive revolution (1950s+): return to mental processes as legitimate science; the book notes Chomsky’s influence against radical behaviorist accounts of language.
  • History also covers diversity in the field (including barriers faced by early women psychologists) and multicultural / cross-cultural critiques—e.g., risks of overgeneralizing from narrow (often WEIRD) samples (≈p. 16).

1.3 Contemporary Psychology §1.3, ≈pp. 18–26

  • Many subdivisions: biopsychology/evolutionary, sensation & perception, cognitive, developmental, personality, social, and applied areas.
  • Applied examples: industrial-organizational (I-O), health, sport & exercise, forensic, clinical psychology.
  • APA as a major professional organization with many divisions reflecting this diversity.

1.4 Careers in Psychology §1.4, ≈pp. 26–30

  • Academic research careers usually need a doctorate; master’s opens other applied options; bachelor’s skills transfer widely.
  • PhD vs PsyD: PsyD leans more applied/clinical; PhD often more research-oriented.
  • Prescribing medication is typically a physician (psychiatrist) role in most U.S. states—not a standard clinical psychologist credential.

Further Theoretical Depth

External journals and classic books that go beyond OpenStax’s sketches. Citations are named sources—not the textbook restated.

Chomsky’s review of Skinner’s Verbal Behavior argued that stimulus–response chaining cannot explain the creativity and structure of language; the critique helped reopen “mind” as a scientific object for psychology and linguistics.

Chomsky, N. (1959). A review of B. F. Skinner’s Verbal Behavior. Language, 35(1), 26–58. doi.org/10.2307/411334

這篇書評常被視為認知轉向的重要催化，而不只是「反對行為主義」口號。

Rogers’s client-centered framework treats the therapeutic relationship—congruence, empathy, and unconditional positive regard—as the active change mechanism, not technique alone. That is a fuller clinical theory than a one-line textbook mention of humanism.

Rogers, C. R. (1951). Client-Centered Therapy. Houghton Mifflin. Google Books. See also Rogers, C. R. (1961). On Becoming a Person. Houghton Mifflin. Google Books

Henrich, Heine, and Norenzayan’s WEIRD critique shows that many “universal” findings come from Western, Educated, Industrialized, Rich, Democratic samples and often fail cross-cultural tests—so sampling bias is a validity problem, not only a diversity slogan.

Henrich, J., Heine, S. J., & Norenzayan, A. (2010). The weirdest people in the world? Behavioral and Brain Sciences, 33(2–3), 61–83. doi.org/10.1017/S0140525X0999152X

延伸書中對跨文化／WEIRD 的提醒：樣本偏差會系統性扭曲「人類心理」結論。

Science-as-framework note (philosophy of science, not a psychology experiment): Kuhn described science as working under shared paradigms that periodically shift; Popper stressed falsifiability as a demarcation ideal. Useful for reading “schools of psychology” as research programs, not as timeless truths.

Kuhn, T. S. (1962/1970). The Structure of Scientific Revolutions. University of Chicago Press. University of Chicago Press; also UChicago Press. Popper, K. (1959). The Logic of Scientific Discovery (English ed.). Hutchinson. Routledge. (Framing labels—distinct from OpenStax’s historical narrative.)

Must-know contrasts

High-yield comparisons for this chapter. Focus on common confusions and everyday examples. Paraphrased from OpenStax Psychology 2e ideas plus named classics; not verbatim textbook text.

Must-know contrast table

Structuralism vs functionalism

ConceptCore ideaCommon confusionEveryday example
Structuralism (Wundt/Titchener)Break conscious experience into elements via introspectionWriting that Wundt’s whole program was “just structuralism,” or treating structuralism and functionalism as two names for the same lab methodIn a methods seminar you try to list every sensation in “the taste of iced coffee” (bitter, cold, sweet) instead of asking why you drink it before a 9 a.m. lecture
Functionalism (James)How mental processes help the organism adaptAssuming functionalism means “modern evolutionary psychology only,” so any adaptive story automatically counts as JamesYou ask why craving coffee before a midterm is useful (alertness for studying) rather than cataloguing the drink’s flavor notes

Psychoanalytic vs behaviorism

ConceptCore ideaCommon confusionEveryday example
Freud / psychoanalyticUnconscious drives, early conflict, clinical methodsClaiming Freud founded psychology as a science, or that every psychology course is secretly FreudianYou call a classmate “Mom” by accident and a friend insists it must mean a hidden wish about your mother
Behaviorism (Watson/Skinner)Study observable behavior; mind often sidelinedSaying behaviorists claim people never learn or never think—when the real claim was to study observable behavior rigorouslyYou earn a coffee stamp for every completed practice set and start opening the quiz app without being nagged

Correlation vs causation

ConceptCore ideaCommon confusionEveryday example
CorrelationVariables move togetherConcluding ice cream “causes” drowning because both rise in summer (a third variable—heat—drives both)Your class survey shows students sleeping under 6 hours also report higher stress—linked scores, not proof sleep loss caused the stress
Causation (experiment)Manipulating one variable produces change in anotherTreating a large r as if it already proves “X makes Y happen” without manipulation/controlResearchers randomly assign some students to a short-sleep night and then measure next-day stress ratings

Theory vs evidence

Theory / claimSupporting evidenceLimits / challenges
Introspection can map the structure of consciousness (structuralist hope)Early lab work did standardize some reporting; it pushed psychology toward controlled observation (OpenStax §1.2)Reports are subjective, hard to verify across observers; later schools largely abandoned pure introspection
Unconscious processes shape everyday behavior (Freud)Clinical observations of hysteria/neurosis and defense patterns influenced therapy culture for decadesMany claims hard to falsify; modern evidence for specific Freudian mechanisms is mixed or weak
Psychology should be an objective science of behavior (radical behaviorist stance)Powerful learning laws from conditioning labs; applied behavior analysis still usedLanguage, memory strategies, and mental imagery need cognitive constructs Chomsky and others revived
