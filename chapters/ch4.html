Key Notes

Textbook concepts from OpenStax Psychology 2e (paraphrased). Sleep, drugs, and altered states that appear in the book belong here.

4.1 What Is Consciousness? §4.1, ≈pp. 110–114

  • Consciousness = awareness of internal and external stimuli; sleep vs wakefulness and other states on a continuum.
  • Circadian rhythms (~24 h) strongly entrained by light; master clock in the SCN (hypothalamus).
  • Melatonin from the pineal rises in darkness; sleep debt accumulates with insufficient sleep.
  • Jet lag and rotating shift work misalign circadian timing; bright-light therapy is discussed as one remediation approach.

4.2 Sleep and Why We Sleep §4.2, ≈pp. 114–116

  • Adaptive/evolutionary accounts and restorative / cognitive (learning–memory) accounts of why sleep matters.
  • Hormones related to growth/maturation are secreted during sleep; insufficient sleep impairs cognition, mood, and health.
  • Sleep is both homeostatically regulated (debt accumulates) and circadian-gated (timing windows)—weekend “catch-up” only partly fixes chronic misalignment.

4.3 Stages of Sleep §4.3, ≈pp. 116–120

  • NREM: Stage 1 (transition; alpha → theta) → Stage 2 (sleep spindles & K-complexes) → Stage 3 deep slow-wave (delta).
  • REM: rapid eye movements, vivid dreaming likely, muscle atonia; EEG more wake-like.
  • Cycles across the night: more deep sleep early; longer REM toward morning; both NREM and REM linked to learning/memory in the text.
  • Dream theories in the book: Freud’s manifest vs latent content; Cartwright’s continuity with waking concerns; Hobson’s activation-synthesis / protoconsciousness framing.

4.4 Sleep Problems and Disorders §4.4, ≈pp. 120–126

  • Insomnia: difficulty falling or staying asleep.
  • Parasomnias: unwanted motor/experiential events—sleepwalking, night terrors (often NREM-related), REM sleep behavior disorder (RBD), restless legs.
  • Sleep apnea (obstructive vs central; CPAP common for OSA); narcolepsy (sleep attacks; often cataplexy).

4.5 Substance Use and Abuse §4.5, ≈pp. 126–134

  • Tolerance and physical/psychological dependence; withdrawal risks.
  • Classes: depressants (alcohol, barbiturates, benzodiazepines), stimulants (caffeine, nicotine, amphetamines, cocaine), opioids, hallucinogens, cannabis/marijuana (legal status varies).
  • Alcohol’s early “sociable” feel does not make it a stimulant pharmacologically; combining depressants stacks risk.

4.6 Other States of Consciousness §4.6, ≈pp. 134–136

  • Hypnosis: focused attention and suggestibility; dissociation vs social-cognitive role accounts (not mystical mind control).
  • Meditation: trained focus/awareness (including mindfulness); studied for stress, mood, and related outcomes.

中文補充：REM 是「腦相對活躍＋肌肉張力低」；夢遊多與深 NREM 有關。Alcohol 在藥理分類上是抑制劑（depressant）。

Further Theoretical Depth

External sleep and consciousness research that extends beyond the textbook’s sketch of dream theories, deprivation, and meditation outcomes.

Hobson and McCarley’s activation-synthesis model proposed that dreaming reflects the forebrain’s attempt to make a narrative from periodic brainstem activation during REM—shifting dream theory from wish-fulfillment toward a neurocognitive account.

Hobson, J. A., & McCarley, R. W. (1977). The brain as a dream state generator: An activation-synthesis hypothesis of the dream process. American Journal of Psychiatry, 134(12), 1335–1348. doi.org/10.1176/ajp.134.12.1335

延伸書中對 activation-synthesis 的簡述：核心是脑干活化＋前脑整合，而非只記名詞。

Cartwright’s program treats dreams as continuous with waking emotional concerns and as contributing to overnight mood regulation—an empirical “dream function” line rather than a purely Freudian latent-content story.

Cartwright, R. (2010). The Twenty-four Hour Mind: The Role of Sleep and Dreaming in Our Emotional Lives. Oxford University Press. Oxford University Press (Builds on her earlier clinical/research papers on dreaming and mood.)

Rechtschaffen’s controlled sleep-deprivation work in animals demonstrated that prolonged total sleep loss is biologically catastrophic—strong evidence that sleep is a physiological necessity, not a cultural luxury.

Rechtschaffen, A., Gilliland, M. A., Bergmann, B. M., & Winter, J. B. (1983). Physiological correlates of prolonged sleep deprivation in rats. Science, 221(4606), 182–184. doi.org/10.1126/science.6857280

Jouvet’s classic brainstem work linked REM (paradoxical) sleep to pontine mechanisms and dissociated muscle atonia from the EEG signature of REM—foundational for modern REM neurobiology.

Jouvet, M. (1967). Neurophysiology of the states of sleep. Physiological Reviews, 47(2), 117–177. doi.org/10.1152/physrev.1967.47.2.117

Clinical meditation reviews find modest average benefits for anxiety, depression, and pain in some populations, with important caveats about study quality and expectancy—useful realism beyond “meditation always heals.”

Goyal, M., et al. (2014). Meditation programs for psychological stress and well-being: A systematic review and meta-analysis. JAMA Internal Medicine, 174(3), 357–368. doi.org/10.1001/jamainternmed.2013.13018

ConceptCore ideaCommon confusionEveryday example

Homeostatic sleep drivePressure builds with time awakeIgnoring sleep debt because you “feel fine” at the wrong clock timeCrashing mid-afternoon when debt and the circadian dip combine

Theory vs evidence

Theory / claimSupporting evidenceLimits / challenges
Sleep serves restoration / adaptive functionsSleep deprivation impairs cognition and mood; slow-wave and REM linked to different restorative roles (OpenStax §4.3)No single function explains all sleep; species differences complicate “one purpose” theories
Hypnosis is an altered state with genuine demand + dissociation componentsSuggestibility effects are replicable for pain and memory reports in some paradigmsDebate remains: special state vs social-role compliance; not a reliable “memory truth serum”
Psychoactive drugs act on neurotransmitter systems to alter consciousnessAgonist/antagonist pharmacology maps onto stimulant, depressant, opioid, hallucinogen classesExpectancy, set/setting, and polydrug use change real-world effects beyond receptor charts
