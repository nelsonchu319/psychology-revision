Key Notes

Textbook concepts from OpenStax Psychology 2e (paraphrased).

7.1 What Is Cognition? §7.1, ≈pp. 214–217

  • Cognition: thinking and related processes (perceive, know, remember, judge, decide).
  • Concepts group similar information; prototypes are best examples of a category.
  • Schemata are clusters of related concepts (incl. role and event schemata / scripts).

7.2 Language §7.2, ≈pp. 218–221

  • Language = lexicon + grammar; phonemes → morphemes → semantics & syntax.
  • Acquisition follows a similar early sequence worldwide; critical/sensitive period themes appear in the chapter.
  • Linguistic influences on thought (Sapir–Whorf themes) remain debated.

中文補充：語言結構（音素／語素／語法）與「語言是否形塑思考」是兩條常考線。

7.3 Problem Solving §7.3, ≈pp. 222–227

  • Strategies: trial-and-error, algorithms, heuristics, breaking problems into subgoals.
  • Obstacles: mental set, functional fixedness, anchoring, confirmation bias, hindsight bias, representative & availability heuristics.

7.4 Intelligence and Creativity §7.4, ≈pp. 228–229

  • Spearman’s g; Cattell crystallized vs fluid; Sternberg’s triarchic (analytical, creative, practical); Gardner’s multiple intelligences; emotional intelligence.
  • Creativity: divergent thinking / novel useful ideas (hard to measure objectively).

7.5 Measures of Intelligence §7.5, ≈pp. 230–235

  • History: Binet, Stanford–Binet, Wechsler scales; IQ scoring and norming; Flynn effect.
  • Bell curve; intellectual disability and giftedness ranges as framed in the chapter; reliability/validity concerns; cultural bias debates.

7.6 The Source of Intelligence §7.6, ≈pp. 236–246

  • Nature and nurture both contribute; heritability estimates; range of reaction; socioeconomic and educational contexts.
  • Learning disabilities (e.g., dyslexia, dysgraphia) vs intellectual disability distinctions in the text.

Further Theoretical Depth

External journals and classic books beyond the textbook sketch.

Kahneman and Tversky’s heuristics-and-biases program shows how availability and representativeness can systematically distort judgment under uncertainty.

Tversky, A., & Kahneman, D. (1974). Judgment under uncertainty: Heuristics and biases. Science, 185(4157), 1124–1131. doi.org/10.1126/science.185.4157.1124

Spearman’s early psychometric work grounded the idea of a general intelligence factor (g) extracted from positive manifold among tests.

Spearman, C. (1904). “General intelligence,” objectively determined and measured. American Journal of Psychology, 15(2), 201–292. doi.org/10.2307/1412107

Gardner’s multiple intelligences and Sternberg’s triarchic model challenge narrow IQ-only definitions—useful contrasts for exam essays.

Gardner, H. (1993). Frames of Mind (10th anniversary ed.). Basic Books. Google Books. Sternberg, R. J. (1985). Beyond IQ: A Triarchic Theory of Human Intelligence. Cambridge University Press. doi.org/10.1017/CBO9780511611476 (CUP; access may vary).

Alternate Sternberg overview: Sternberg, R. J. (1984). Toward a triarchic theory of human intelligence. Behavioral and Brain Sciences, 7(2), 269–287. doi.org/10.1017/S0140525X00044629

Chomsky’s nativist framing of language acquisition contrasts with pure reinforcement accounts of verbal behavior.

Chomsky, N. (1959). Review of B. F. Skinner’s Verbal Behavior. Language, 35(1), 26–58. doi.org/10.2307/411334

Must-know contrasts

High-yield comparisons for this chapter. Focus on common confusions and everyday examples. Paraphrased from OpenStax Psychology 2e ideas plus named classics; not verbatim textbook text.

Must-know contrast table

Fluid vs crystallized intelligence

ConceptCore ideaCommon confusionEveryday example
Fluid intelligenceNovel problem-solving, reasoning speedClaiming older adults lose “all intelligence,” ignoring preserved/grown crystallized knowledgeYour grandparent struggles with a brand-new logic puzzle app they’ve never seen
Crystallized intelligenceAccumulated knowledge/skillsEquating crystallized intelligence with trivia night facts alone, or calling it “not real intelligence”They instantly define obscure words on a crossword from decades of reading

Algorithm vs heuristic

ConceptCore ideaCommon confusionEveryday example
AlgorithmStep-by-step procedure guaranteeing a solution (if one exists)Calling every shortcut or rule of thumb an “algorithm” on an examIn a new supermarket you walk every aisle systematically until you find soy sauce
HeuristicMental shortcut; fast, sometimes biasedAssuming heuristics always equal irrational errors rather than useful shortcuts that can biasYou go straight to “Asian foods” for soy sauce and usually succeed—fast, not guaranteed

Reliability vs validity (testing)

ConceptCore ideaCommon confusionEveryday example
Reliability (of an IQ/achievement test)Score stabilityTreating a stably high IQ score as proof the test validly measures personal worth or every talentYou retake a practice aptitude test next week and land within a few points of last time
Validity (of an IQ/achievement test)Predicts what it claims (e.g., academic performance)Treating school-predictive validity as validity for creativity, morality, or “life success” overallA test that predicts GPA can still miss studio-art creativity or teamwork skill

Theory vs evidence

Theory / claimSupporting evidenceLimits / challenges
g / general intelligence underlies cognitive test correlations (Spearman tradition)Positive manifold across diverse cognitive tests is robustDoes not capture all valued abilities; debate on multiple intelligences vs g
Heuristics save effort but create systematic biases (Tversky & Kahneman)Representativeness, availability, anchoring effects widely replicatedLab wording can exaggerate bias; expertise and incentives sometimes reduce errors
IQ tests can be reliable predictors of some academic/work outcomesModerate correlations with school performance; standardization improves fairness vs informal judgmentsCultural bias, stereotype threat, and narrow constructs limit interpretation; IQ ≠ destiny
