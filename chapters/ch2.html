Key Notes

Textbook concepts from OpenStax Psychology 2e (paraphrased). Design/ethics ideas that appear in the book belong here.

2.1 Why Is Research Important? §2.1, ≈pp. 36–40

  • Research beats intuition, authority, and luck for evaluating complex claims (including media/technology effects).
  • Deductive reasoning: theory → hypothesis → observations/tests; inductive: observations → broader ideas.
  • A theory is a well-developed set of ideas that explain observed phenomena; a hypothesis is a testable prediction.
  • Operational definitions specify how variables will be manipulated or measured so studies can be replicated.

2.2 Approaches to Research §2.2, ≈pp. 40–48

  • Clinical/case study: rich depth on one or few people; weak generalizability.
  • Naturalistic observation: behavior in real settings; limited control; watch observer bias; inter-rater reliability helps.
  • Surveys: many respondents; wording, social desirability, and honesty matter; distinguish sample vs population and the value of a random sample.
  • Archival research: existing records; no control over original data collection.
  • Longitudinal vs cross-sectional: same people over time vs compare groups at one time (cohort effects matter for cross-sectional comparisons).

2.3 Analyzing Findings §2.3, ≈pp. 48–58

  • Correlation coefficient r (−1 to +1): direction and strength; correlation ≠ causation (third variables / directionality).
  • Illusory correlations: perceiving relationships that are not there.
  • Experiments: manipulate independent variable (IV), measure dependent variable (DV); experimental vs control groups; random assignment; control confounds; placebos; single-blind / double-blind designs to limit expectancy and experimenter bias.
  • Random assignment ≠ random sampling: assignment supports causal comparison within the sample; sampling supports generalizing to a population.
  • Statistical significance (often α = .05 framing in the text) is not automatic practical importance; replication and peer review matter.
  • Reliability (consistency) vs validity (measuring the intended construct)—a measure can be reliable yet invalid.

2.4 Ethics §2.4, ≈pp. 58–62

  • IRB reviews human-participant proposals; informed consent; minimize harm; confidentiality/privacy.
  • Deception only when justified, with full debriefing afterward.
  • Animal work reviewed by an IACUC; humane care and scientific justification.

Further Theoretical Depth

External design and ethics sources that extend beyond the textbook’s sketch of experiments, confounds, and IRBs.

Campbell and Stanley’s classic typology of experimental and quasi-experimental designs catalogs threats to internal validity (history, maturation, selection, instrumentation, regression, etc.) and shows why “having a control group” is not enough without knowing which threats remain open.

Campbell, D. T., & Stanley, J. C. (1963). Experimental and Quasi-Experimental Designs for Research. Rand McNally. Internet Archive. (Later expanded in Shadish, W. R., Cook, T. D., & Campbell, D. T. (2002). Experimental and Quasi-Experimental Designs for Generalized Causal Inference. Houghton Mifflin.)

把「相關≠因果」升級成：具體列出哪些效度威脅還未排除。

The Belmont Report articulates respect for persons, beneficence, and justice as ethical principles for human-subjects research—institutional IRB practice is downstream of this policy framework, not merely a local checklist.

National Commission for the Protection of Human Subjects of Biomedical and Behavioral Research. (1979). The Belmont Report. U.S. Department of Health, Education, and Welfare. hhs.gov Belmont Report

The Open Science Collaboration’s multi-lab project re-ran a large set of psychology studies and found substantially lower replication rates than original reports—fueling today’s emphasis on preregistration, open data, and effect-size humility.

Open Science Collaboration. (2015). Estimating the reproducibility of psychological science. Science, 349(6251), aac4716. doi.org/10.1126/science.aac4716

Meehl argued that soft-psychology theories often make only weak, directional predictions; “significant vs not” under a vague null then advances theory slowly. Stronger theories should risk more precise (risky) predictions.

Meehl, P. E. (1967). Theory-testing in psychology and physics: A methodological paradox. Philosophy of Science, 34(2), 103–115. doi.org/10.1086/288135. See also Meehl, P. E. (1978). Theoretical risks and tabular asterisks: Sir Karl, Sir Ronald, and the slow progress of soft psychology. Journal of Consulting and Clinical Psychology, 46(4), 806–834. doi.org/10.1037/0022-006X.46.4.806

Must-know contrasts

Must-know contrast table

Independent vs dependent variable

ConceptCore ideaCommon confusionEveryday example
Independent variable (IV)What the researcher manipulatesCalling the measured score the IV, or listing every demographic as “the IV”Assigning some students to spaced study vs cramming
Dependent variable (DV)What is measured as the outcomeTreating the DV as something the researcher “sets” rather than recordsMeasuring exam score after the study-method assignment

Reliability vs validity

ConceptCore ideaCommon confusionEveryday example
ReliabilityConsistency of a measureAssuming a reliable test is automatically valid (a broken scale can be reliably wrong)A mood app gives the same score every morning
ValidityWhether it measures what it claimsEquating face appeal of a quiz with construct validityThe same app tracks battery life, not mood (reliable but not valid)

Experimental vs correlational design

ConceptCore ideaCommon confusionEveryday example
ExperimentalManipulate IV, control, random assignment → causal claimCalling any study with numbers an “experiment”Randomly assigning a meditation app vs waitlist
CorrelationalMeasure relationships without manipulationTreating a survey correlation as proof that X causes YSeeing that meditators report less stress (link only)

Theory vs evidence

Theory / claimSupporting evidenceLimits / challenges
Experiments support causal inference when well controlledRandom assignment + control groups reduce confounds; classic lab and field experiments illustrate this (OpenStax §2.2–2.3)Lab control can hurt ecological validity; ethics/feasibility block many manipulations
Operational definitions make constructs measurableClear ops allow replication and comparison across studiesNarrow ops can miss the construct (measuring “intelligence” only as one timed quiz)
Ethical codes protect participants (IRB, informed consent, deception limits)Post–WWII and post–Milgram reforms; APA ethics; institutional review (OpenStax §2.4)Trade-offs: mild deception sometimes used; online research raises new consent/privacy issues
