Key Notes

Textbook concepts from OpenStax Psychology 2e (paraphrased).

14.1 What Is Stress? §14.1, ≈pp. 484–493

  • Stress as process: appraise events as overwhelming/threatening; health psychology studies psychological influences on health.
  • Cannon fight-or-flight; HPA axis; Selye’s General Adaptation Syndrome—alarm, resistance, exhaustion.

14.2 Stressors §14.2, ≈pp. 494–499

  • Chronic vs acute stressors; traumatic events; life changes (SRRS / LCUs—Holmes & Rahe); daily hassles; job strain / job burnout.

14.3 Stress and Illness §14.3, ≈pp. 500–511

  • Psychophysiological disorders; immunosuppression themes; cardiovascular disease & Type A hostility components; asthma; tension headaches as discussed.

中文補充：壓力→疾病不是單純「想太多」，而是評估、生理軸線與行為路徑交織。

14.4 Regulation of Stress §14.4, ≈pp. 512–517

  • Coping: problem-focused vs emotion-focused; perceived control; social support; stress-reduction techniques (exercise, meditation, biofeedback, relaxation).

14.5 The Pursuit of Happiness §14.5, ≈pp. 518–534

  • Positive psychology; happiness factors (adaptation, affective forecasting limits); flow; optimism; practices that relate to well-being as in the chapter.

Further Theoretical Depth

External journals and classic books beyond the textbook sketch.

Selye’s GAS remains the textbook physiological stress sequence even as modern models add appraisal and recovery.

Selye, H. (1950). Stress and the general adaptation syndrome. British Medical Journal, 1(4667), 1383–1392. doi.org/10.1136/bmj.1.4667.1383

Lazarus and Folkman’s transactional model centers cognitive appraisal and coping type.

Lazarus, R. S., & Folkman, S. (1984). Stress, appraisal, and coping. Springer. Google Books

Cohen and colleagues’ work links perceived stress and social ties to immune and illness outcomes.

Cohen, S., Tyrrell, D. A., & Smith, A. P. (1991). Psychological stress and susceptibility to the common cold. New England Journal of Medicine, 325(9), 606–612. PubMed 1719106

Seligman’s positive psychology agenda reframes health psychology toward strengths, optimism, and well-being—not only pathology.

Seligman, M. E. P., & Csikszentmihalyi, M. (2000). Positive psychology: An introduction. American Psychologist, 55(1), 5–14. doi.org/10.1037/0003-066X.55.1.5
