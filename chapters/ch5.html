Key Notes

Textbook concepts from OpenStax Psychology 2e (paraphrased). Important ideas that appear in the book belong here—not in Further Depth.

5.1 Sensation versus Perception §5.1, ≈pp. 146–148

  • Sensation: sensory receptors detect stimulus energy.
  • Perception: organizing, interpreting, and consciously experiencing those sensations (bottom-up and top-down).
  • Absolute threshold: minimum stimulus energy detected ~50% of the time; difference threshold / just noticeable difference (jnd): minimum difference detected ~50% of the time (Weber’s law relates jnd to stimulus magnitude).
  • Sensory adaptation, selective attention, and signal detection help explain what we notice vs. miss (including inattentional blindness examples).

中文補充：感覺是「偵測」，知覺是「組織＋解讀」；閾值多以約 50% 偵測率定義。

5.2 Waves and Wavelengths §5.2, ≈pp. 149–152

  • Light and sound described as waves: amplitude, wavelength, frequency, timbre.
  • Longer wavelength ↔ lower frequency; shorter ↔ higher.
  • Vision: wavelength ↔ color; amplitude ↔ brightness.
  • Hearing: frequency ↔ pitch; amplitude ↔ loudness.

5.3 Vision §5.3, ≈pp. 153–160

  • Path: cornea → pupil → lens focuses on retina (especially fovea).
  • Cones: acuity, bright light, color; concentrated in fovea. Rods: dim light, periphery.
  • Optic nerve → optic chiasm (partial crossing) → occipital processing.
  • Color: trichromatic (three cone types) + opponent-process (pairs; afterimages).
  • Depth: binocular and monocular cues.

5.4 Hearing §5.4, ≈pp. 161–164

  • Canal → eardrum → ossicles → oval window → cochlea fluid → hair cells on basilar membrane → auditory nerve.
  • Pitch: place + rate coding on the basilar membrane.
  • Localization: monaural and binaural cues.
  • Conductive vs sensorineural hearing loss.

5.5 The Other Senses §5.5, ≈pp. 164–168

  • Chemical senses: gustation and olfaction.
  • Touch/temperature/pain via skin receptors and free nerve endings; nociception for pain.
  • Vestibular sense (balance: utricle, saccule, semicircular canals); proprioception / kinesthesia for body position and movement.

5.6 Gestalt Principles of Perception §5.6, ≈pp. 168–172

  • Figure–ground; grouping by proximity and similarity; good continuation; closure.
  • Perceptions are fallible; culture, bias, and expectations (perceptual set) shape what we see.

Further Theoretical Depth

External journals and classic books that go beyond OpenStax’s sketches.

Signal detection theory separates sensitivity from response bias—showing that “threshold” is not a single fixed point but a decision under noise.

Green, D. M., & Swets, J. A. (1966). Signal Detection Theory and Psychophysics. Wiley.

The classic inattentional blindness demonstration (gorilla among basketball passes) shows that attention, not just receptor stimulation, gates awareness.

Simons, D. J., & Chabris, C. F. (1999). Gorillas in our midst: Sustained inattentional blindness for dynamic events. Perception, 28(9), 1059–1074.

書中提及相關現象；這篇是經典實驗論文，適合放 Depth。

Feature-integration theory proposes that focused attention binds separable features into objects—linking cognitive attention research to perceptual errors.

Treisman, A. M., & Gelade, G. (1980). A feature-integration theory of attention. Cognitive Psychology, 12(1), 97–136.

Gibson’s ecological approach emphasizes rich environmental information (affordances) rather than constructing perception only from impoverished sensations.

Gibson, J. J. (1979). The Ecological Approach to Visual Perception. Houghton Mifflin.
