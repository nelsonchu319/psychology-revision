Key Notes

Textbook concepts from OpenStax Psychology 2e (paraphrased).

15.1–15.3 Definition, DSM, Perspectives §15.1–15.3, ≈pp. 536–545

  • Disorder ≈ significant disturbance in thoughts/feelings/behaviors reflecting dysfunction, with distress/impairment—not merely atypical or culturally expected reactions.
  • DSM-5 classification; comorbidity; labeling pros/cons; supernatural → biological → diathesis–stress / biopsychosocial perspectives.

15.4–15.6 Anxiety, OCD, PTSD §15.4–15.6, ≈pp. 546–557

  • Specific phobia, social anxiety, panic disorder/agoraphobia, GAD; learning and cognitive pathways.
  • OCD (obsessions/compulsions), body dysmorphic disorder, hoarding; PTSD after trauma—intrusions, avoidance, negative cognitions/mood, arousal changes.

15.7–15.8 Mood & Schizophrenia §15.7–15.8, ≈pp. 558–571

  • Major depressive disorder; bipolar disorder; biological & cognitive vulnerability themes.
  • Schizophrenia: positive vs negative symptoms; dopamine / neural hypotheses as presented; genetic risk.

中文補充：分清「正性症狀（幻覺／妄想）」與「負性症狀（情感淡漠／無動機）」。

15.9–15.11 Dissociative, Childhood, Personality §15.9–15.11, ≈pp. 572–596

  • Dissociative amnesia, depersonalization/derealization, dissociative identity disorder (controversial prevalence/etiology debates).
  • ADHD, autism spectrum disorder themes; personality disorders in clusters (A odd, B dramatic, C anxious)—e.g., borderline, antisocial as highlighted.

Further Theoretical Depth

External journals and classic books beyond the textbook sketch.

Wakefield’s harmful-dysfunction analysis influenced how modern definitions combine value (harm) and scientific (dysfunction) judgments.

Wakefield, J. C. (1992). Disorder as harmful dysfunction: A conceptual critique of DSM-III-R’s definition of mental disorder. Psychological Review, 99(2), 232–247. doi.org/10.1037/0033-295X.99.2.232

Rosenhan’s “On being sane in insane places” remains a classic (and contested) critique of psychiatric labeling and hospital context.

Rosenhan, D. L. (1973). On being sane in insane places. Science, 179(4070), 250–258. doi.org/10.1126/science.179.4070.250

Beck’s cognitive model of depression anchors cognitive vulnerability and later CBT.

Beck, A. T. (1967). Depression: Clinical, experimental, and theoretical aspects. Harper & Row. archive.org. Article: Beck, A. T. (2008). The evolution of the cognitive model of depression. American Journal of Psychiatry, 165(8), 969–977. doi.org/10.1176/appi.ajp.2008.08050721

Gottesman and others’ behavioral-genetics summaries support diathesis–stress thinking for schizophrenia liability.

Gottesman, I. I. (1991). Schizophrenia genesis: The origins of madness. Freeman. archive.org. Review: Sullivan, P. F., Kendler, K. S., & Neale, M. C. (2003). Schizophrenia as a complex trait. Archives of General Psychiatry, 60(12), 1187–1192. PubMed 14662550 (doi:10.1001/archpsyc.60.12.1187)

Must-know contrasts

High-yield comparisons for this chapter. Focus on common confusions and everyday examples. Paraphrased from OpenStax Psychology 2e ideas plus named classics; not verbatim textbook text.

Must-know contrast table

Distress vs dysfunction

ConceptCore ideaCommon confusionEveryday example
DistressSubjective sufferingPathologizing any unusual behavior or intense feeling without asking about impairment/distressIntense sadness for days after a breakup—painful, but context matters for disorder labels
DysfunctionImpairment in life domains (plus cultural deviance considerations)Treating every intense emotion as a disorder even when work/relationships still functionThe same sadness also stops you attending lectures and answering messages for weeks

Fear vs anxiety

ConceptCore ideaCommon confusionEveryday example
FearPresent-focused alarm to a specific threatUsing fear and anxiety as perfect synonyms in clinical write-upsYou jump and freeze when a car nearly hits you in a crosswalk—present threat
AnxietyAnticipatory apprehension about future threatTreating weeks of future-oriented worry as identical to panic at a present threatFor weeks you worry “something will go wrong” before an exam that hasn’t happened yet

Obsessions vs compulsions

ConceptCore ideaCommon confusionEveryday example
ObsessionsIntrusive unwanted thoughts / images / urgesCalling any neat desk preference “OCD,” or confusing obsessions with enjoyably chosen hobbiesAn intrusive, unwanted doubt that you left the stove on ruins the start of a lecture
CompulsionsRepetitive behaviors / mental acts aimed at reducing distressTreating any repeated habit (playlist on loop) as a clinical compulsionYou leave the flat, walk back, and check the burners five times to cut the doubt

Theory vs evidence

Theory / claimSupporting evidenceLimits / challenges
DSM categories improve communication and research reliabilityStructured criteria reduce purely idiosyncratic labels (OpenStax §15.2)Comorbidity high; categories can reify continuous symptoms; cultural critique continues
Diathesis–stress: vulnerability + stressor → disorder likelihoodIntegrates biology and environment; fits many mood/anxiety patternsSpecific diatheses often unknown; risk is probabilistic not deterministic
Anxiety disorders involve learning + cognitive biases + biologyConditioning, attentional bias to threat, and heritability evidence convergeHeterogeneity across GAD, phobia, panic; one mechanism doesn’t fit all
